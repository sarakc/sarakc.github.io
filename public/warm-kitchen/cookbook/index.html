The Warm Kitchen

A pescatarian cookbook from the world's warming traditions, for a cold, dry, blood-poor, hypermobile constitution

The traditions

Each tradition below has its own name for this constitution and its own reasons, but they arrive at the same plate: warm, cooked, oily, gently spiced food, eaten at regular times. All of them describe the same type, cold, thin, pale, dry, tense and prone to catching colds, and prescribe these dishes for it. The Why note under each recipe gives the reason in that tradition's own terms.

Ayurveda (India). Calls this vata: the dosha of air and space, which is cold, dry, light, rough and always moving. Too much vata shows as a thin frame, dry skin, cracking joints, cold hands, light sleep, worry and an unsteady appetite. The cure is the opposite qualities: warm, moist, heavy, oily, soft-cooked food with sweet, sour and salty tastes, ghee above everything, and ginger, cumin and cardamom to keep agni, the digestive fire, steady. Raw, cold, dry and very bitter foods are what to avoid.

Chinese medicine. Reads this as Spleen qi and Kidney yang deficiency with weak wei qi, the defensive qi at the surface: cold limbs, tiredness, loose joints, and colds that settle in the Lung as phlegm. The Spleen dislikes cold and damp, so food is cooked, warm and easy to digest, with congee as the base. Ginger, scallion, cinnamon and walnuts warm yang; black sesame, goji and red dates nourish the blood that feeds the sinews, which the Liver governs. Where the dryness runs deep, it reads as yin deficiency, and millet, black beans, oysters, seaweed, black sesame and dark berries nourish the yin. A weak Spleen also lets dampness collect, which adzuki, barley, celery, ginger and green tea drain.

Taiwanese food tonics (shibu). Taiwan cooks within Chinese medicine but with kitchen habits of its own. Shibu, eating as tonic, is how families look after one another, and jinbu, the tonic eating that starts at Lidong in early November, readies the body for winter. Both lean on three ingredients: black sesame oil, mature ginger and rice wine. The same three feed women through zuo yuezi, the month of rest after birth, when cold food and cold water are kept away.

Tibetan medicine (Sowa Rigpa). Calls this rlung, the wind energy and close kin of vata. Rlung disorders bring restlessness, poor sleep, dizziness and a cold, depleted body. The standard prescription is warm, oily, nourishing food: butter tea, tsampa, bone broth (or fish broth), molasses, garlic and nutmeg.

Nepali home medicine. Nepal's kitchens sit where Ayurveda and Tibetan medicine meet, and everyday speech sorts foods as hot or cold. A new mother is kept warm for weeks on ghee, carom-seed broth and spiced sweets, and the same warming seeds, ajwain, cumin and fenugreek, go into the pot when the monsoon brings colds.

Persian and Unani medicine. Sort people and foods by temperament, mizaj: hot or cold, wet or dry. This body is cold and dry, so the kitchen leans on warm and moist foods such as dates, honey, saffron, cinnamon, almonds, walnuts and ghee. Persian cooks balance every dish, which is why cold ingredients like yogurt, barley and rice arrive with saffron, turmeric, cinnamon or fried mint. Greek medicine, which Unani grew from, uses the same four qualities.

Swahili coast kitchens. On the coasts of Kenya and Tanzania, centuries of Indian Ocean trade brought cardamom, cloves, ginger and cinnamon into African cooking, and with them the Arab and Indian habit of sorting foods as hot or cold. The everyday answers to a cold or a long recovery are a ginger tea, a millet porridge and a pot of beans in coconut milk.

Korean medicine (Hanbang). Its Sasang system would call this type Soeumin: cold digestion, a sensitive stomach, quick to tire and to worry. Soeumin food is warming and gentle: ginger, jujube, sesame oil, glutinous and short-grain rice, and soft porridges (juk). Korean households add seaweed soup for anyone rebuilding after illness or birth.

Japanese food wisdom (yōjō and kampo). Less a system of types than a habit of care: warm the body from the stomach, eat small and regular meals, and meet a cold early with ginger, daikon, kudzu, umeboshi and soft rice. Kampo, Japan's herbal medicine, treats the first chill of a cold with kakkonto, a formula built on kudzu root and ginger.

Filipino folk medicine. Filipino households sort foods and activities as hot or cold, and pasma is the illness said to follow a sudden shift between them, such as washing in cold water after hard work: trembling hands, aching joints, numbness. The remedy is warmth from the inside, and the kitchen gives it in ginger broths. New mothers get tinola with moringa leaves for their milk and strength.

Indonesian jamu. Jamu is Indonesia's herbal medicine, still sold door to door in Java as fresh drinks of turmeric, ginger and tamarind. Its most familiar complaint is masuk angin, "wind has entered": chills, aches, a bloated stomach and a cold coming on after rain or a late night. The cure is warmth from inside, in ginger drinks and in broths and stews thick with galangal, lemongrass and turmeric.

Andean medicine (Quechua and Aymara). The Kallawaya healers of highland Bolivia picture the body as a set of channels through which blood, fat and water must keep moving; illness is a flow that has stopped, or runs too fast or too slow. Hot and cold, wet and dry describe that flow, and cold, dry blood is tied to coughs and chest complaints. Fat is prized against the cold altiplano nights, and losing it is treated as an illness of its own. Spanish herbals were folded into this after the conquest, and how much was already there is still debated. In the kitchen it means thick soups of potato, quinoa, squash and corn, and a hot spiced corn drink before dawn.

Mesoamerican medicine (Nahua, Maya and Zapotec). Most Indigenous peoples of Mexico, the Nahua and Yucatec Maya best documented among them, sort foods, herbs and illnesses as hot or cold and treat each with its opposite. A body that has just given birth is cold and is warmed with hot food and the temazcal, the steam bath; a menstruating woman keeps away from cold food and cold water. Scholars now read the system as a blend of Mediterranean humoral medicine and the older Mesoamerican idea of a world held together by paired opposites. Its kitchen is the milpa, the field of corn, beans and squash, seasoned with epazote and achiote.

Caribbean bush medicine. Across the Caribbean, home medicine runs on bush teas and restorative broths, drawn from African, Indigenous, Indian and European practice. A cold is met with fish tea, ginger and thyme, and Puerto Rican and other Spanish-speaking Caribbean households also sort foods as hot or cold, as Mexico does. The warming spices are allspice, which Jamaica grows as pimento, thyme, nutmeg and a whole Scotch bonnet floated in the pot.

Native North American kitchens. There is no single system here. There are hundreds of nations, each with its own teachings, and many of them say simply that food is medicine: a relationship with the land, eaten in season, set against what replaced it in the reservation era, commodity flour, sugar and lard. Haudenosaunee farmers grow the Three Sisters, corn, beans and squash, together in one field; Diné cooks stir juniper ash into blue corn mush; and for the Anishinaabe, manoomin, wild rice, is the food that grows on water, which their migration story says marks their homeland. Buy from Native producers when you can.

Cold and highland kitchens. The Russian, Hungarian, Nordic and Georgian dishes, and those from the Ethiopian highlands, don't come from a medical system. They come from places where cold weather set the menu: fish soups, butter, dill, rye, walnuts, spiced butter and long-simmered pots.

How to use this book

Three rules

Everything warm and cooked. Fat and protein in every dish. Spice every pot.

The six jobs

Every recipe does at least one of six jobs, marked in its title. The tags always appear in this order, so a recipe's first tag is not its main one; read them as a set.

  • WARM heats a cold body: ginger, cinnamon, pepper, mustard, broths, long cooking.
  • OIL moistens dry tissue and nourishes the sinews: ghee, sesame, olive oil, coconut, butter, tahini, nuts.
  • BUILD gives protein and minerals for muscle, bone and sinew: fish, eggs, legumes, seaweed, sesame, tofu.
  • BLOOD gives iron and the foods every tradition uses to build blood: clams, mussels, oysters, sardines, egg yolk, lentils, dark greens, black sesame, molasses, goji, jujube, longan. Lemon, lime or tomato in the pot helps the iron absorb; tea and milk an hour away from it does the opposite.
  • PHLEGM transforms mucus, drip and the dampness behind them: ginger, daikon, mustard, scallion, black pepper, shiitake, adzuki, barley, celery.
  • CALM settles a scattered nervous system: warm milk, nutmeg, cardamom, saffron, dates, jujube, slow soups.

The Index by job at the back lists every recipe under each tag.

The weeknight plate

A starch, a protein, a vegetable and a sauce. Cook rice or another grain. Salt the fish and sear it in ghee 3–4 min a side, or steam it 8 min on a bed of ginger; cook shrimp in a hot pan with garlic 2–3 min. Then pick a vegetable from chapter 7 and a sauce from chapter 8. Change those two and the same rice and fish make a different dinner every night.

Slow days

Reach for the freezer. Broth, congee, kitchari, dal, borscht and misir wot all freeze in single portions, and ginger grated into an ice tray means no knife. Tinned sardines or clams over rice with Ginger-Scallion Oil, or a bowl of Gyeran-jjim, is dinner in ten minutes. Sipping Broth from a jar in the fridge takes three. Next day notes under some recipes turn one batch into a second meal.

Conventions

  • Every recipe serves 2–4 unless it says otherwise. Cook times are approximate. Salt to taste throughout.
  • Where a recipe gives two times, such as "10 min active, 2 hours on its own", only the first needs you at the stove; for the rest the pot simmers or soaks by itself.
  • Ingredients marked optional are traditional extras; every recipe works without them.
  • Ingredients are listed in the order they go into the pot, separated by dots.
  • Oven temperatures are given in both scales.
  • A capitalized dish name (Dashi, Sesame Spinach, Tahini Sauce) is a recipe elsewhere in the book; the document outline finds it.
  • ◇ marks a dish that pairs fish or shellfish with milk, cream or yogurt. Ayurveda counts that as an incompatible pair (viruddha ahara), so skip these if you follow that rule.
  • ○ marks a recipe that cooks honey. Ayurveda says not to heat honey; swap in sugar, jaggery or maple if you follow that rule. Honey stirred in off the heat, as in the drinks, is fine by that rule.
  • Everything can be doubled and frozen except the fish and shellfish mains and the egg dishes. When in doubt, add ghee.

Chapter 1Foundations

Things the other recipes depend on. Make the fats and spice blends once a season and the broths every Sunday.

GheeOIL

Melt 1 lb unsalted butter over low heat. Simmer 20–25 min without stirring until the foam subsides, the milk solids brown at the bottom, and it smells nutty. Strain through cheesecloth into a jar. Keeps months at room temperature.

Why: the fat Ayurveda puts above every other for vata; it moistens, grounds and carries the spices into the food.

Niter Kibbeh (Ethiopian spiced butter)WARMOIL

Makes about 1½ cups. 1 lb unsalted butter · ¼ onion, chopped · 3 garlic cloves · thumb of ginger, sliced · 1 tsp fenugreek seed · 1 tsp cumin seed · 4 cardamom pods, crushed · 1 tsp dried oregano · ½ tsp turmeric · 1 cinnamon stick

  1. Melt butter on low; add the rest.
  2. Barely simmer 30–40 min, without stirring, until clear and fragrant.
  3. Strain into a jar. Fridge 1 month.

Why: ghee with the warming spices already in it. In Ayurvedic terms, fat to settle vata and spice to keep agni lit. Use it anywhere a recipe calls for ghee.

Ayurvedic Spice BlendWARM

2 tbsp cumin · 2 tbsp coriander · 1 tbsp fennel seed · 1 tbsp turmeric · 1½ tsp black pepper · 1½ tsp ginger powder. Grind. Use 1–2 tsp per pot.

Why: the vata-pacifying spices in one jar: cumin, coriander and fennel to help digestion, pepper and ginger to warm it.

Persian AdviehWARMCALM

1 tbsp cinnamon · 1 tbsp cardamom · 2 tsp cumin · 1 tsp coriander · ½ tsp nutmeg · ½ tsp cloves · a pinch of dried rose petals if you have them. Mix. For rice and stews.

Why: every spice here is warm and sweet in Persian medicine, which is how Persian cooks temper cold rice and legumes.

Fish and Kombu BrothWARMBUILD

Makes 2 quarts. The pescatarian bone broth. 1–2 lb fish frames and heads (gills removed, rinsed) · 4-inch piece kombu · 1 onion · 2 celery stalks · 1 carrot · thumb of ginger, sliced · 1 tsp peppercorns · 1 tbsp cider vinegar · 2½ qt cold water

  1. Everything except kombu in cold water. Bring slowly to a bare simmer; skim.
  2. Simmer gently 40–45 min (never boil; longer turns bitter).
  3. Kombu in for the last 10 min; remove.
  4. Strain, salt lightly. Fridge 4 days; freeze in jars.

Next day: Ginger Congee with Egg from 5 cups; Tibetan Thukpa from the rest.

Why: Tibetan medicine prescribes bone broth for rlung, and the vinegar pulls the minerals out of the frames; kombu adds what fish bones lack.

DashiBUILDPHLEGM

Makes 1 quart. Base for miso, nimono, thukpa, udon. 4-inch piece kombu · 4 dried shiitake · 1 qt water · optional: handful of bonito flakes

  1. Soak kombu and shiitake in cold water 30 min (or overnight).
  2. Heat slowly; remove kombu just before boiling. Simmer shiitake 10 min.
  3. Off heat, add bonito if using; steep 3 min; strain. Slice the shiitake for the soup.

Why: the Japanese stock that opens nearly every warm meal of the day; shiitake and kombu are both Lung and mineral foods in kampo and Chinese medicine.

Sipping BrothWARMBUILD

1½ cups Fish and Kombu Broth or Dashi · 1 tsp grated ginger · 1 tsp miso, fish sauce or tamari · salt · scallion, or a few drops of sesame oil. Heat the broth with the ginger 3 min; miso off the heat; salt until it tastes like the sea. A mug between meals, or in place of a meal on a bad day. Keep broth in jars in the fridge and this takes three minutes.

Why: warm, salted fluid is the oldest prescription for a cold, faint body in every tradition here; butter tea, clam broth, miso and dashi are all doing this job.

Chapter 2Porridges, Congees and Eggs

The first meal sets the digestion for the day, so every tradition here starts it warm, soft and a little fatty. Congees and porridges first, then the egg dishes, which are also the fastest dinners in the book.

Ginger Congee with Egg (China)WARMBUILDCALM

½ cup jasmine rice, rinsed · 5 cups Fish and Kombu Broth (or 4 water + 1 broth) · thumb of ginger in matchsticks · ½ tsp salt · 2 eggs · 2 tsp sesame oil · 2 scallions · white pepper

  1. Rice, broth, ginger, salt to a boil; lowest simmer, uncovered.
  2. Cook 60–75 min, stirring every 15, until silky. Add hot water if thick. (Or slow cooker overnight on low.)
  3. Crack eggs into the hot congee and stir until just set.
  4. Finish with sesame oil, scallions, white pepper. Add flaked fish for lunch.

Why: the Chinese convalescent breakfast; warm, easy to digest, ginger clears drip.

Ch&aacute;o C&aacute; (Vietnamese fish congee)WARMBUILDPHLEGM

½ cup jasmine rice · 6 cups Fish and Kombu Broth or water · thumb of ginger, half sliced, half in matchsticks · ½ lb white fish, sliced thin · 1 tbsp fish sauce · 2 shallots, fried crisp in 2 tbsp oil · scallions, dill, black pepper

  1. Toast rice in a dry pot 3 min. Broth and sliced ginger; simmer, partly covered, 45 min, stirring now and then.
  2. Fish sauce. Off heat, fish slices on top; cover 3 min.
  3. Shallots and their oil, ginger matchsticks, scallions, dill, lots of pepper.

Tainan style: cook the rice in the broth only until tender, 15–20 min, so the grains stay whole in a clear soup, and finish with diced celery and the fried shallots. Made with milkfish belly (sold frozen and boneless at Asian groceries), it is the breakfast of Tainan, shi mu yu du zhou.

Why: what Vietnamese families cook for a cold. Like Chinese medicine, Vietnamese folk medicine uses rice porridge to rebuild and pepper, ginger and scallion to drive out chill.

Tinutuan (Bubur Manado, Indonesia)WARMBUILDBLOOD

10 min active, 45 min on its own. ½ cup jasmine rice · 6 cups water · 2 cups kabocha or butternut, peeled and diced · 1 sweet potato, peeled and diced · 1 cup corn kernels (frozen is fine) · 1 stalk lemongrass, bruised · thumb of ginger, sliced · 3 cups spinach · 1 cup basil leaves · salt · to serve: fried or grilled fish, tinned mackerel or a fried egg, lime, fried shallots, fish sauce · optional: water spinach (kangkung) or cassava leaves for the greens; lemon basil (kemangi); sambal on the side

  1. Rice, water, squash, sweet potato, corn, lemongrass and ginger to a boil; simmer, partly covered, 35–40 min, stirring now and then, until the rice and squash melt together. Add hot water if it gets thick.
  2. Spinach and basil 2 min. Lift out the lemongrass and ginger. Salt.
  3. Into bowls; fish or egg on top, lime, shallots and a few drops of fish sauce.

Why: the breakfast porridge of Manado in North Sulawesi, thick with squash, corn and greens and eaten with fried fish. Lemongrass and ginger warm the pot, the greens and fish build blood, and the lime helps the iron absorb.

Millet and Kabocha Porridge (Xiaomi Nangua Zhou, China)WARMBUILDCALM

¾ cup millet, rinsed · 2 cups kabocha or butternut, peeled and diced · thumb of ginger, sliced · 6 cups water · pinch of salt · 1 tbsp goji berries · 1 tsp ghee or sesame oil · black sesame

  1. Toast the millet in a dry pot 2 min, until it smells nutty.
  2. Squash, ginger, water and salt; boil, then lowest simmer, partly covered, 35–40 min, stirring now and then, until the squash melts into the grain.
  3. Goji for the last 5 min. Ghee and black sesame in the bowl.

Next day: loosen with hot broth and stir in a beaten egg for a savory bowl.

Why: the everyday breakfast of northern China, and what new mothers there are fed for a month after birth. Chinese medicine counts millet as cool and moistening, the grain for a dry, tired Stomach, so the ginger and squash are there to keep it warm.

Eight-Treasure Congee (Babao Zhou, China)BUILDBLOODCALM

½ cup short-grain or jasmine rice · ¼ cup black rice · ¼ cup adzuki beans · 6 dried jujubes · 8 dried longan · 2 tbsp walnuts · 2 tbsp peanuts or lotus seeds (optional) · 7 cups water · 2 tbsp goji berries · brown sugar or jaggery

  1. Soak the rice, black rice and adzuki overnight if you can.
  2. Everything but the goji and sugar in a pot; simmer 60–75 min, stirring now and then, or slow cooker overnight on low.
  3. Goji for the last 10 min. Sweeten in the bowl; a spoon of Black Sesame Paste on top.

Why: the classic Chinese blood-nourishing porridge, eaten at the Laba festival and in convalescence. Jujube, longan and goji nourish blood; black rice and adzuki are Kidney foods.

Red Bean and Barley Congee (Hong Dou Yi Mi Zhou, China)WARMBUILDPHLEGM

½ cup adzuki beans · ½ cup pearl barley, or Job's tears (yi yi ren) · thumb of ginger, sliced · 2 dried jujubes, slit · 7 cups water · pinch of salt · 1 tbsp goji berries · Gomasio

  1. Soak the beans and barley overnight; drain.
  2. Everything but the goji and gomasio in a pot; simmer, partly covered, 75–90 min, stirring now and then, until the beans split and the barley turns creamy. Or slow cooker overnight on low.
  3. Goji for the last 10 min. Eat it savory with gomasio, or with honey stirred in off the heat.

Why: the porridge Chinese households make when the body feels heavy and damp. Adzuki and barley are the first foods Chinese medicine reaches for to drain dampness (Job's tears is the classic grain; pearl barley is the kitchen stand-in), and since both are cool, the ginger and jujube warm the pot.

Sweet Potato Congee (Digua Zhou, Taiwan)WARMBUILDCALM

10 min active, 45 min on its own. ¾ cup short-grain or jasmine rice, rinsed · 1 large sweet potato (about 1 lb), peeled and cut in 1-inch chunks · thumb of ginger, sliced · 7 cups water · pinch of salt · to serve: fried or soft-boiled eggs, a few drops of sesame oil, Gomasio · optional: Taiwanese yellow sweet potato, fermented tofu (doufu ru) on the side

  1. Rice, sweet potato, ginger, water and salt to a boil; lowest simmer, partly covered, 40–45 min, stirring now and then, until the rice blooms and the potato is soft. Add hot water if it gets thick.
  2. Into bowls; an egg on top, sesame oil and gomasio over. A little fermented tofu on the side if using; it is very salty.

Quicker: simmer 2 cups leftover rice with the sweet potato, ginger and 4 cups water 15 min.

Why: the congee of Taiwan's lean postwar years, when sweet potato stretched the rice, and still the breakfast people make when they want comfort. Chinese medicine counts sweet potato as sweet and nourishing to the Spleen; the ginger keeps the pot warm and the egg makes it a meal.

Jat-juk (Korean pine nut porridge)OILBUILDCALM

½ cup short-grain rice, soaked 1 hour · ½ cup pine nuts · 4 cups water · pinch of salt · honey · 2 dried jujubes, sliced (optional)

  1. Blend drained rice with 1 cup water until fine. Blend pine nuts with 1 cup water.
  2. Simmer the rice with the remaining 2 cups water, stirring, 10 min until thick.
  3. Pine nut milk in; 5 min more, stirring. Salt, honey, jujube.

Why: Korea's classic porridge for convalescents and elders. In Korean and Chinese medicine, pine nuts moisten the Lung and intestines and nourish a thin, dry body.

Hobakjuk (Korean pumpkin porridge)BUILDCALM

1 lb kabocha or butternut, peeled and diced · 3 cups water · ½ cup cooked or canned adzuki beans, drained · ⅓ cup glutinous (sweet) rice flour whisked into ½ cup cold water · pinch of salt · 2 tbsp sugar, or honey off the heat · 1 tbsp pine nuts · optional: small sweet rice balls (saealsim), made from sweet rice flour and hot water and simmered in the porridge 3 min; 2 dried jujubes, sliced

  1. Squash and water; simmer, covered, 15 min until very soft. Mash or blend smooth.
  2. Beans in; stir in the rice flour water and salt; simmer, stirring, 5 min until thick and glossy.
  3. Sugar, or honey off the heat. Pine nuts on top.

Why: Korea's porridge for convalescents and new mothers, smooth enough for the weakest stomach. Korean households give pumpkin after birth for swelling and tiredness, and the adzuki beans and pine nuts make it a meal.

Tsampa Porridge (Tibet)WARMBUILDBLOODCALM

½ cup tsampa (roasted barley flour; or toast barley flour in a dry pan 5 min) · 1½ cups hot Tibetan Butter Tea or hot milk · 1 tbsp butter or ghee · pinch of salt · handful of raisins or chopped dried figs · 1 tsp blackstrap molasses (optional)

  1. Put tsampa in a bowl. Pour in hot tea or milk, stirring, until thick porridge.
  2. Stir in butter and fruit. Eat with a spoon or knead into balls (pa).

Why: the Tibetan staple for cold altitude and rlung; barley and butter build and ground.

Cardamom Oat Porridge (Ayurveda)OILCALM

1 cup rolled oats · 2 cups whole milk or oat milk · 1 cup water · 1 tbsp ghee · ½ tsp cinnamon · ¼ tsp cardamom · pinch of salt · 3 chopped dates · 8 almonds soaked overnight, skinned

  1. Cook oats in milk and water with spices and salt, 8–10 min, stirring.
  2. Stir in ghee and dates at the end. Top with almonds.

Why: sweet, heavy, oily; the textbook vata breakfast.

Rye Porridge (Scandinavia)WARMBUILDCALM

1 cup rye flakes (or half oats) · 3 cups milk or water · pinch of salt · 1 tbsp butter · ½ tsp cinnamon · ¼ tsp cardamom · Stewed Fruit with Cardamom · chopped walnuts

  1. Soak rye in the liquid overnight if you can.
  2. Simmer with salt 12–15 min (20 if unsoaked), stirring.
  3. Butter, spices, fruit, walnuts.

Why: the Nordic winter breakfast; heavy, sweet and buttery, which is exactly what Ayurveda asks of a vata morning.

Amaranth Porridge with Blueberries (Mexico)OILBUILDBLOOD

½ cup amaranth · ½ cup rolled oats · 3 cups water or milk · pinch of salt · ½ tsp cinnamon · 1 cup Blåbärssoppa, or blueberries, blackberries or mulberries simmered 5 min with a slice of ginger · 1 tbsp ground flaxseed or wheat germ · 1 tsp flaxseed or almond oil · pumpkin seeds

  1. Amaranth in the liquid with the salt; simmer, covered, 15 min, stirring once or twice.
  2. Oats and cinnamon; 8 min more, stirring, until thick and glossy.
  3. Warm berries over. Flaxseed and oil off the heat; pumpkin seeds on top.

Why: the warm drink Swedes hand to skiers on the Vasaloppet; the ginger adds extra warmth. Cooking the berries makes them warm and soft, the way Ayurveda wants fruit for vata; it also makes a warm berry compote for porridge.

These take longer or ask a little more skill: a ferment, a dough, a steamed parcel. Most of the time is hands-off waiting, and most make a batch that keeps or freezes, so one session covers several meals. Start them on a slow Saturday.

Injera (Ethiopia)BUILDBLOOD

Makes 8–10 rounds. 2–3 days fermenting, about 45 min cooking. 3 cups (about 360 g) teff flour, brown or ivory · 3½–4 cups lukewarm water (filtered if the tap water is heavily chlorinated) · ½ tsp salt · optional: ⅛ tsp active dry yeast for a first batch; 2–3 tbsp starter liquid saved from the last batch in place of the yeast

  1. Whisk the flour with 3 cups of the water (and the yeast) to a smooth, thick batter. Cover loosely and leave somewhere warm, 70–80°F / 21–27°C.
  2. Ferment 2–3 days, stirring once a day, until it smells clearly sour and is full of bubbles. A layer of grey-yellow liquid on top is normal; fuzzy pink, green or black spots are mold, and the batch goes out.
  3. Pour off the liquid on top; keep 2–3 tbsp in the fridge as a starter for next time.
  4. Optional, for more bubbles: boil 1 cup water, whisk in ½ cup of the batter and stir 2–3 min until thick as pudding. Cool to lukewarm, stir it back into the batter and rest 30–60 min until bubbly.
  5. Add the salt, then thin with water until the batter is like heavy cream.
  6. Heat a 12-inch nonstick skillet with a lid over medium heat, no oil. Pour about ½ cup batter in a spiral from the edge inward and tilt to fill the gaps. When holes open all over the surface and no wet batter remains, cover 1–2 min until the top is dry and the edges lift. Don't flip.
  7. Slide onto a clean towel; cool completely before stacking.

If it goes wrong: no holes means the batter is too thick, under-fermented or the pan too cool; a gummy middle needs a thinner batter or another minute under the lid; brittle edges mean the pan is too hot. All-teff injera is delicate, and replacing a quarter of the teff with barley or all-purpose flour makes it easier to handle.

Keeping: wrapped, 2 days at room temperature, 1 week in the fridge, or frozen with parchment between the rounds. Warm in a covered pan or wrapped in a damp towel before serving with Misir Wot, Shiro or Teff Porridge with Niter Kibbeh.

Why: Ethiopia's daily bread, torn and used to scoop up every wot. Fermenting makes teff easier to digest and frees more of its iron and calcium, which are among the highest of any grain.

Momo in Jhol (Tibet and Nepal)WARMBUILDPHLEGM

Makes about 30. 1½ hours, most of it folding. Dough: 2 cups all-purpose flour, ¾ cup water, pinch of salt · filling: ½ lb shrimp, finely chopped, 2 cups finely chopped cabbage, salted 10 min and squeezed dry, 2 scallions, 2 garlic cloves and a thumb of ginger, grated, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tbsp sesame oil, ¼ tsp black pepper · jhol: 1 tbsp oil, 2 tomatoes, 2 tbsp toasted sesame seeds, 1 garlic clove, thumb of ginger, ½ tsp cumin, ¼ tsp turmeric, 3 cups Fish and Kombu Broth, salt, lemon, cilantro · optional: a pinch of timur in the jhol; firm white fish or crumbled tofu in place of the shrimp

  1. Mix the dough until smooth and firm; cover and rest 30 min. Mix the filling.
  2. Jhol: char the tomatoes in a dry pan 6–8 min. Blend with the sesame, garlic, ginger, cumin, turmeric and 1 cup of the broth. Fry the purée in the oil 3 min; the rest of the broth; simmer 10 min. Salt, lemon.
  3. Roll the dough into a log, cut 30 pieces and roll each into a thin 3-inch circle. A heaped teaspoon of filling in the middle; pleat and pinch closed.
  4. Steam on oiled racks or cabbage leaves 10–12 min, until the wrappers turn glossy.
  5. Momos into bowls, hot jhol over, cilantro.

Next day: freeze uncooked momos on a tray, then bag them; steam from frozen 14–15 min.

Why: the dumplings of Tibet and Nepal, made in big batches for winter, festivals and long cold evenings. The jhol of charred tomato, sesame, ginger and cumin turns them into a hot soup, the kind of warm, oily, spiced food Tibetan medicine prescribes for rlung.

Pesarattu (Andhra mung bean crepes)WARMBUILD

Makes 8. Soak overnight; 30 min cooking. 1 cup whole green mung beans · 2 tbsp rice · thumb of ginger · 1 green chili (optional) · 1 tsp cumin seed · ½ tsp salt · ¾ cup water · ghee for the pan · 1 onion, finely chopped · Cilantro-Ginger Chutney or Coconut-Turmeric Sauce to serve · optional: split mung in place of whole for a softer, quicker-cooking crepe; a spoon of upma (savory semolina) folded inside, as in Andhra

  1. Soak the mung beans and rice in plenty of water 8 hours or overnight; drain.
  2. Blend with the ginger, chili, cumin, salt and water to a smooth batter like thick cream.
  3. Heat a nonstick skillet on medium; rub with a little ghee. Pour ⅓ cup batter in the middle and spread outward in circles with the back of the ladle. Scatter onion over; drizzle ghee around the edge.
  4. Cook 2–3 min until the bottom is golden and the edges lift; flip 1 min. Fold and serve hot with the chutney or sauce.

Next day: the batter keeps 2 days in the fridge; thin with a splash of water before cooking.

Why: the breakfast crepe of Andhra Pradesh, made from mung, the legume Ayurveda counts as the easiest to digest. Soaking and grinding make it lighter still, and ginger and cumin keep agni steady.

Amazake (Japanese warm rice drink)WARMCALM

Makes about 6 cups. 30 min active, 8–10 hours keeping warm. 1 cup short-grain rice · 3 cups water · 7 oz (200 g) dried rice koji (kome koji) · 1 cup warm water · to serve: grated ginger, pinch of salt · optional: a rice cooker, slow cooker or multicooker with a yogurt setting makes the warm hold easy; a thermometer

  1. Cook the rice with the 3 cups water into a soft porridge, 20–25 min. Cool to 140°F / 60°C, warm but not hot to the touch.
  2. Break up the koji and stir it in with the warm water.
  3. Hold at 135–140°F / 57–60°C for 8–10 hours: a rice cooker on keep-warm with the lid open and a towel over it, a slow cooker on warm with the lid ajar, or a yogurt setting. Stir every couple of hours. It is ready when it tastes clearly sweet.
  4. Blend smooth if you like. To serve, heat a cup with an equal amount of water, a pinch of salt and grated ginger; don't boil.

Keeping: 1 week in the fridge, or frozen in portions.

Why: the hot, sweet rice drink served at Japanese shrines at New Year and sold at winter stalls, nicknamed a drinkable IV for how it restores a tired body. Koji turns the rice starch to sugar with no added sweetener, and the ginger keeps it warming.

Black Sesame Tangyuan in Ginger Syrup (China)WARMOILBLOODCALM

Makes about 20. 1 hour. Filling: ½ cup black sesame seeds, toasted and finely ground, 3 tbsp sugar, 3 tbsp soft butter · dough: 1½ cups glutinous (sweet) rice flour, about ⅔ cup hot water · syrup: 3 cups water, 2 thumbs ginger, sliced, 3 tbsp dark brown sugar, 4 dried jujubes, slit · optional: lard in place of the butter, as in the traditional filling; dried osmanthus flowers in the syrup

  1. Mix the filling; chill 20 min until firm, then roll into 20 small balls.
  2. Stir the hot water into the flour a little at a time and knead to a soft, smooth dough like play dough. Divide into 20.
  3. Flatten each piece into a cup, set a filling ball inside, and close and roll smooth.
  4. Syrup: simmer the water, ginger, sugar and jujubes 15 min.
  5. Boil the tangyuan in a separate pot of water; when they float, 2 min more. Lift into bowls and ladle the ginger syrup over.

Next day: freeze uncooked tangyuan on a tray, then bag them; cook from frozen, 2–3 min longer.

Why: eaten across China at the winter solstice (Dongzhi) and the Lantern Festival, the round shape standing for a family together. Black sesame is the classic food for Kidney and Liver blood, and the ginger syrup warms a sweet that would otherwise sit heavy.

Yaksik (Korean sweet rice with jujubes)OILBLOODCALM

Serves 6–8. Soak 4 hours; about 1½ hours cooking. 2 cups glutinous (sweet) rice · ⅓ cup dark brown sugar · 2 tbsp sesame oil · 1 tbsp soy sauce · 1 tsp cinnamon · 10 dried jujubes, pitted and sliced · ½ cup roasted chestnuts (jarred or vacuum-packed), quartered · 3 tbsp pine nuts · 2 tbsp raisins · optional: 1 tbsp honey brushed over at the end, off the heat; a few slices of dried persimmon

  1. Soak the rice 4 hours or overnight; drain.
  2. Steam over boiling water in a cloth-lined steamer 30 min, until tender and sticky. Or cook in a rice cooker with 1⅓ cups water.
  3. While hot, mix in the sugar, sesame oil, soy and cinnamon, then the jujubes, chestnuts, pine nuts and raisins.
  4. Steam again 30–40 min (or run the rice cooker again) until it turns a deep caramel brown.
  5. Press into an oiled dish, cool 15 min and slice. Eat warm.

Keeping: 3 days wrapped at room temperature, or frozen in slices; warm through before eating.

Why: yak means medicine in Korean, and this sweet is eaten at Daeboreum, the first full moon of the lunar year. Jujube, pine nuts and sesame oil are all Hanbang foods for a cold, tired body and thin blood.

Tamales de Rajas con Queso (Mexico)WARMOILBUILD

Makes about 16. 1 hour assembling, 1¼ hours steaming. 20 dried corn husks · masa: 2 cups masa harina, 1 tsp baking powder, 1 tsp salt, 1¾ cups warm broth, ⅔ cup soft butter · filling: 3 poblano peppers, roasted, peeled and cut in strips, 1 onion, sliced and softened in oil, 8 oz Oaxaca cheese or Monterey Jack, in strips, ½ cup Cooked Salsa Roja · optional: lard in place of the butter, as most Mexican cooks use; salsa verde in place of the roja

  1. Soak the husks in hot water 30 min.
  2. Mix the masa harina, baking powder, salt and broth to a soft dough. Beat the butter until fluffy, 3 min, then beat in the dough a little at a time, 3–4 min more. A small piece should float in a glass of water.
  3. Spread 3 tbsp masa over the wide half of each husk. Lay poblano, onion, cheese and a spoon of salsa down the middle; fold the sides over, then fold the narrow end up.
  4. Stand the tamales open end up in a steamer basket over boiling water. Cover with spare husks and a lid; steam 60–75 min, adding boiling water as needed, until the masa pulls cleanly from the husk.
  5. Rest 10 min before unwrapping.

Keeping: freeze cooked tamales in their husks; steam from frozen 20–25 min.

Why: the festival food of Mexico, made in batches with family for Candelaria, Day of the Dead and Christmas, and eaten hot with a cup of atole. Steaming gives moist, heavy warmth, and the butter and cheese make it a meal.

Dhalpuri Roti (Trinidad and Tobago)OILBUILD

Makes 8. 1½ hours. Filling: 1 cup yellow split peas, ½ tsp turmeric, 4 garlic cloves, 1½ tsp ground cumin, ½ tsp salt · dough: 3 cups all-purpose flour, 2 tsp baking powder, ½ tsp salt, about 1¼ cups water · ghee or oil for cooking · optional: a hot pepper in the filling; a cast-iron tawa

  1. Boil the split peas with the turmeric 15–20 min, until tender but holding their shape. Drain well and spread out to dry 15 min.
  2. Grind the peas with the garlic, cumin and salt to a dry, fine powder in a food processor.
  3. Mix the dough until soft and smooth; rest 30 min. Divide into 8 balls.
  4. Flatten a ball into a cup, fill with 3 tbsp of the pea mixture and pinch closed. Rest 10 min, then roll gently into a thin 9-inch round, flouring as needed.
  5. Cook on a hot dry skillet 1 min, flip, brush with ghee; 1 min, flip, brush again, 30 s, until speckled. Keep warm wrapped in a towel.

Keeping: 2 days wrapped in the fridge, or frozen with parchment between; warm in a dry pan.

Why: the roti Indian families brought to Trinidad, with ground split peas rolled inside so the bread carries its own protein. Cumin and garlic warm it, ghee keeps it soft, and it is eaten with curry, especially Maharagwe ya Nazi or Kerala Fish Molee.
